01 Studio philosophy

We believe a building should be the slowest thing in a town — outlasting its fashions, its budgets and its architects. Timber lets us build that way: warm to the hand, light on the earth, and honest about its age.

i.

Grown, not extracted

Every beam we specify comes from managed Nordic forests within 400 km of the site. Trees that took eighty years to stand become buildings meant to stand two hundred.

ii.

Light is a material

At 63° north the sun is low and generous in summer, and scarce in winter. We shape sections, aperture and surface to catch every degree of it — and to glow from within when it is gone.

iii.

Joinery over fixings

We prefer the dovetail to the bracket. Honest joints are visible, repairable and teachable — so the next generation of carpenters can maintain what we drew.

03 How we work

From onewalk in theforest to araised frame.

01Listen
  1. 01

    Listen & walk the site

    We begin with weeks, not drawings: tea with the people who will use the building, a day on the ground in every season we can manage, and a map of where the wind, water and winter sun actually go.

    Weeks 1–6
  2. 02

    Select the timber

    Before a plan exists, we choose the forest. Species, grade and grain direction decide span, section and character — and our sawyers tag the trees that will become the primary frame.

    Weeks 6–12
  3. 03

    Draw by hand, then by machine

    Sketch models in spruce offcuts, then full parametric models for the CNC joinery. Every connection is drawn once for the carpenter and once for the person who will repair it in 2090.

    Months 3–9
  4. 04

    Raise the frame

    Prefabricated in our workshop, the frame goes up in days. The first beam is always raised with the client and a toast of birch sap — the building is topped out before it is closed in.

    Months 10–16
  5. 05

    Return, every year

    We come back after the first winter, then every fifth year. We oil the cladding, listen to the building, and update its care manual. A Norr building is never finished — only handed on.

    Ever after
“A tree is a slow clock. We simply build a house around its time.”
